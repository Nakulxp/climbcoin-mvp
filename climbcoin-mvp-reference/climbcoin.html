The cost of the default

Nobody chose the lift. It was just already there.

Lifts are the overburdened default in almost every tall building. Stairwells sit beside them — zero wait, zero energy, already built and already paid for — and go unused because they are hidden, unlit, and socially invisible.

5–10Share of total building energy drawn by lift systems

Peak-hour congestion is the worst of it: the morning, lunch and evening surges size the whole system, and everything else runs oversized the rest of the day.

42Seconds — typical peak wait, per trip

Multiplied across a headcount and a working year, waiting becomes one of the largest uncosted line items in a building's operation. It appears on no budget.

15.2Percent — lift from point-of-choice prompts alone

Signage alone measurably moves people. Multi-component programmes reach 22.3%. The behaviour is changeable; nobody has made the change worth repeating.

Our interviews found the barrier is not effort. It is habit and company. People ride up two floors mid-conversation, and skip a half-empty lift rather than stand in it alone. Five floors was never the problem — being the only one on the stairs was.

The system

Tap at the door. Climb. The points are already there.

Three seconds of user effort, twice. Everything else runs underneath.

At the stairwell door

Tap in

A ClimbHub reader sits at the entry. The user holds a phone or band against it. The tap does one job: it stamps where and when, anchoring the climb to your building.

Between the taps

The climb is measured

Barometric elevation, step cadence and elapsed time are read from the device the user already carries. This — not the tap — is the proof that a climb happened.

On tap out

Points settle

Verified floors convert to points in the app wallet, feed the user's streak and their team's standing, and land in your dashboard as building-level data.

ClimbHub — the hardware

A wall or floor-mounted NFC reader, one pair per stairwell. Power over Ethernet, no moving parts, tamper-logged. This is the only thing that gets installed.

The app — what your people see

Balance, daily goal, streak, team standing, and a rewards catalogue. Built cross-platform; reads from Apple Health and Health Connect rather than asking anyone to log anything.

The dashboard — what you see

Peak lift load against baseline, floors climbed by floor and by department, participation and retention curves, estimated energy delta, and a clean export for your sustainability reporting.

Why this one works

Every stair-reward scheme before this one could be beaten by taking the lift.

Scan a code at the bottom, scan a code at the top, collect the reward — with no evidence in between. Once a workforce works that out, the programme is a budget leak with a leaderboard on top. This is the problem worth solving, so we solved it first.

ClimbCoin treats the tap as a location anchor, never as proof. Proof comes from three independent signals that a lift cannot reproduce together.

Signal 01 — Elevation

Every current phone and smartwatch carries a barometric altimeter, and both Apple Health and Health Connect expose floors climbed as a first-class metric. A lift ascent registers as elevation gain with no climbing gait beneath it.

Signal 02 — Cadence

Stair climbing has a distinct accelerometer signature: sustained, rhythmic, vertically loaded. Standing in a lift car has effectively none. Elevation without cadence is a ride, and it scores nothing.

Signal 03 — Plausibility

Eight floors in twenty-five seconds is not a climb. Time-bounding each tap pair against a human range catches the remaining edge cases, and flags anomalies to the dashboard rather than silently paying them out.

The principle

We are not chasing a perfect anti-fraud system. We are clearing a lower and far more useful bar: defeating the verification has to be more effort than simply taking the stairs. Past that point, cheating stops being rational.

It runs on the wearables your people already own

Apple Watch, Wear OS, Fitbit and Garmin all feed the same verification stack through their platform APIs — which is what makes ClimbCoin credible to the fitness-serious staff who drive early adoption and pull everyone else along. For anyone without a smartwatch, a low-cost NFC wristband or keyfob does the tap, and can double as your existing access credential. No proprietary hardware on anyone's wrist, and nothing to replace when their next phone arrives.

The questions you are about to ask

Answered before you have to ask them.

Won't engagement collapse after six weeks?

Points alone, yes — extrinsic novelty reliably fades inside two months. What survives is social and identity-based: department-versus-department standings, streaks with real loss-aversion, and cumulative milestones people come to own. Points buy the first month. Teams and streaks buy the year, and the dashboard shows you the retention curve honestly either way.

Doesn't this penalise people who can't use stairs?

It would, if we rewarded stairs. We reward verified movement — stairs simply carry the highest multiplier. A wheelchair user earns on distance moved; anyone earns on steps. Nobody is excluded from the programme by their body, which also removes what would otherwise be a genuine procurement blocker.

Won't crowded stairwells become a safety problem?

We know precisely when each stairwell is busy, because we are measuring it. Multipliers are reduced at peak and raised off-peak, so the system shapes demand away from the crush instead of adding to it. Over time this makes ClimbCoin the demand-shaping layer for vertical movement in the building, not just a rewards app.

Where does the movement data go?

Individual data belongs to the individual. Employers see aggregate and team-level figures, never one person's floor-by-floor movement log. Opt-in is explicit, opt-out is one tap, and insurance sharing is per-member consent — never a default of the deployment. Getting this wrong once would end the programme, so it is a hard architectural line rather than a policy promise.
